Chapter 10 · Muscle tissue — structure

Deck: Ch 10 Muscle Tissue (135 slides)

3 types of muscle

Ch10 s3–4, s18

  • Skeletal — striated, voluntary, multinucleated, long parallel cells
  • Cardiac — striated, branched, intercalated discs
  • Smooth — no striations
All three have actin and myosin. Only skeletal muscle can produce excess heat (s3, review s18).

Properties of muscle cells

Ch10 s7–8

  • Contractility — proteins draw closer together
  • Excitability — responds to a stimulus with an action potential
  • Conductivity — conducts the action potential along the plasma membrane
  • Extensibility — can be stretched without rupturing
  • Elasticity — returns to original length
  • Durability (labelled “not in textbook”)

Functions of skeletal muscle

Ch10 s6

  • Body movement
  • Maintenance of posture (stabilize joints)
  • Protection and support
  • Regulating ingestion and elimination (sphincters)
  • Heat production

3 connective tissue wrappings (outside → in)

Ch10 s10

  • Epimysium — dense irregular CT around the whole muscle
  • Perimysium — dense irregular CT around a fascicle; many blood vessels and nerves
  • Endomysium — areolar CT around one fiber; insulation, capillary support
Review question s18 asks which layer is the “muscle sheath”. The slide doesn’t say. Epimysium (whole muscle) is the usual answer — that part is mine, not the slide’s.

Muscle → smaller (size order)

Ch10 s9, s16, s26–28

  • Whole muscle (an organ)
  • Fascicle
  • Muscle fiber = muscle cell
  • Myofibril
  • Myofilaments (thick and thin)
Sarcomere = the functional unit of contraction (s27, s34). The myofilament is smaller than the myofibril (s18 Q6).

Special names in a muscle cell

Ch10 s12, s22–25

  • Sarcolemma = plasma membrane
  • Sarcoplasm = cytoplasm
  • Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) = modified ER; stores and releases Ca²⁺
  • T-tubules = extensions of the sarcolemma; carry action potentials into the fiber
  • Terminal cisternae = enlarged SR ends flanking each T-tubule
Triad = 2 terminal cisternae + 1 T-tubule (s25).

Proteins of the sarcomere

Ch10 s28–32, s38

  • Thick filament: myosin
  • Thin filament: actin + tropomyosin + troponin
  • Contractile: myosin, actin
  • Regulatory: tropomyosin (covers actin’s active sites), troponin (holds tropomyosin in place)
  • Connectin = titin: Z disc to M line, springlike
  • Dystrophin: anchors actin to the sarcolemma; defect → muscular dystrophy

Sarcomere bands

Ch10 s34–36

  • I band (light) — thin filaments only
  • A band (dark) — thick filaments, overlaps thin
  • Zone of overlap — where contraction occurs
  • Z disc, M line, H zone
When it contracts: the H zone and I band narrow, Z discs move together, the zone of overlap gets thicker, and no filament shortens (s36).

Neuromuscular junction (NMJ) — 3 parts

Ch10 s57–60

  • Axon terminal (synaptic knob) — vesicles full of acetylcholine
  • Synaptic cleft — the gap
  • Motor end plate — part of the sarcolemma with ligand-gated Na⁺ channels
Neurotransmitter at the NMJ = acetylcholine (ACh). Enzyme that breaks it down = acetylcholinesterase (s65, s79).

Chapter 10 · Electrical signal and contraction

Resting membrane and the pump

Ch10 s45–50

  • Cells are negative inside at rest
  • Muscle resting potential: slide 45 says −85 mV; slide 50 says “about 90 mV”. Know both.
  • Set by Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pump + leak channels (voltage-gated channels closed)
  • Pump needs ATP; more Na⁺ outside, more K⁺ inside
  • Excitable tissues = muscle and nervous tissue only (s48)

3 types of gated channels

Ch10 s51

  • Voltage-gated — open to a voltage change
  • Ligand-gated (chemically gated) — open to a chemical
  • Pressure-gated — skin sensory receptors

Action potential — 2 stages

Ch10 s52–54

  • Depolarization: voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ rushes in, rises to about +30 mV, then they close
  • Repolarization: voltage-gated K⁺ channels open at +30 mV, K⁺ leaves, back to resting
  • Spreads over the sarcolemma, down the T-tubules, which makes the SR release Ca²⁺

Excitation → contraction (9 steps)

Ch10 s76

  • 1 Action potential arrives at NMJ
  • 2 ACh released into the synaptic cleft
  • 3 ACh binds ligand-gated Na⁺ channels on the end plate
  • 4 End-plate potential big enough → action potential in the fiber
  • 5 AP travels down sarcolemma into T-tubule, stimulates the triad
  • 6 SR Ca²⁺ channels open, Ca²⁺ floods in
  • 7 Ca²⁺ binds troponin, uncovers actin’s active sites
  • 8 Myosin attaches, power stroke; repeats while ATP and Ca²⁺ remain
  • 9 No more APs → SR takes Ca²⁺ back (needs a little ATP)

Cross-bridge cycle

Ch10 s71–74

  • Myosin heads rest cocked
  • Bind actin → cross-bridge
  • Power stroke: myosin pulls actin toward the M line
  • New ATP breaks the link with actin and re-cocks the head

Relaxation and rigor mortis

Ch10 s77

  • Motor neuron stops signaling
  • Ca²⁺ pumped back into the SR
  • Connectin stretches the muscle out; antagonists pull it back
  • Rigor mortis ~3–4 h after death: SR leaks Ca²⁺, muscles stay contracted

Drugs and toxins at the NMJ

Ch10 s78

  • Botox — blocks ACh release → paralysis for months
  • Malathion — blocks acetylcholinesterase → tetanic contractions
  • Succinylcholine — fools the esterase → paralysis ~10 min
  • Curare — blocks the ACh receptor → paralysis, death

Chapter 10 · Energy and tension

Muscle energy sources

Ch10 s82–84

  • Stored ATP — 1–2 seconds
  • Creatine phosphate — several seconds
  • Glycogen → glucose → lactate (no oxygen) — 1–5 minutes
  • Aerobic (glucose, then free fatty acids) — hours
  • Myoglobin — extra oxygen store

Which fuel for which race

Ch10 s83

  • 50 m (<10 s) → phosphate (creatine PO₄) system
  • 400 m (<1 min) → glycolysis
  • 1500 m (>1 min) → aerobic, using free fatty acids

Oxygen debt — what the extra oxygen repays

Ch10 s86

  • Replace oxygen on myoglobin
  • Replenish glycogen, ATP, creatine phosphate
  • Convert lactic acid back to glucose or metabolize it
  • Bring pH back to normal

3 periods of a twitch

Ch10 s91

  • Latent — after stimulus, no tension yet
  • Contraction — tension rising
  • Relaxation — tension falling (a bit longer)
Threshold = the minimum voltage that triggers a twitch. Twitch = briefest contraction to a single stimulus.

Repeated stimulation

Ch10 s93–95

  • Wave summation — stimulating again before it fully relaxes; tension rises (Ca²⁺ builds up)
  • Tetanus — stimulated 80–100 times/second; ~100% of maximum tension (unfused → fused)

Slow vs fast fibers

Ch10 s98–101

  • Slow (Type I): smaller, less force for longer, oxidative, many mitochondria, lots of myoglobin, dark/red, postural muscles
  • Fast (Type II): glycolytic, fewer mitochondria, less myoglobin, white

Motor unit, recruitment, tone

Ch10 s104–107

  • Motor unit = one motor neuron + all the fibers it innervates
  • Recruitment = using more motor units for more force
  • Tone = background low-level stimulation, never zero
  • Hypertonia = too much (spasticity); hypotonia = too little (“floppy baby”)

Contraction types

Ch10 s109

  • Isotonic concentric — muscle shortens; muscle force > external force
  • Isotonic eccentric — muscle lengthens; external force > muscle force
  • Isometric — length unchanged, no movement; forces equal
Length–tension (s96): a sarcomere makes the most tension at 100–120% of resting length.

Chapter 10 · Training, aging, smooth and cardiac

Endurance vs resistance training

Ch10 s112–115

  • Endurance (>15 min): ↑ oxidative enzymes, ↑ mitochondria, ↑ blood vessels; uses free fatty acids faster
  • Resistance: ↑ myofibrils and fiber diameter = hypertrophy
  • Training changes fibers inside, not the number of fibers (s112)

Disuse, fatigue and aging

Ch10 s117–118, s123

  • Disuse: ↓ myofibrils and fiber size = atrophy; long paralysis → fibrosis → contractures
  • Fatigue: depleted creatine phosphate, glycogen, glucose; less oxygen; build-up of Ca²⁺, ADP, phosphate; heat
  • Aging (starts about 35): ↓ fiber size and number, ↓ motor neurons, ↓ glycogen and myoglobin, ↓ satellite cells, insulin resistance

Smooth muscle

Ch10 s125–128

  • Peristalsis, sphincters, controls blood and air flow
  • Has actin and myosin, but no sarcomeres, no striations
  • Slide says: no troponin, no tropomyosin
  • Latch-bridge, dense bodies
  • Autonomic control + myogenic response (contracts if stretched)
  • No motor end plates, no T-tubules, little SR
Shared with skeletal: actin and myosin, and it contracts.

Cardiac muscle

Ch10 s132–134

  • Only in the heart; deep red/brown from myoglobin
  • Small, branched cells
  • Intercalated discs link cells electrically and physically
  • Pacemaker cells are autorhythmic (spontaneous action potentials)

Chapter 11 · Skeletal muscles — basics

Deck: Muscles Ch 11 (64 slides). Slide 20 says you must know connections and actions of 13 muscles “indicated on the list”. That list isn’t in this deck. Get it from the course page or the instructor and mark your 13 here.

Fascicle patterns and muscle shapes

Ch11 s6–13

  • Parallel — strap-like
  • Convergent — broad triangle tapering to one tendon
  • Pennate (feather) — unipennate, bipennate, multipennate
  • Sphincter — circular, around openings
  • Spiral — wraps around a bone
  • Fusiform — thick in the middle, tapered ends

Functional groups of muscles

Ch11 s16–17

  • Agonist (prime mover) — most of the force
  • Antagonist — opposite action; controls the agonist
  • Synergist — helps the agonist
  • Fixator — stabilizes by anchoring a bone

Origin, insertion, action

Ch11 s19

  • Origin — anchoring end, usually the more proximal bone, moves least
  • Insertion — the moving end
  • Action — what the muscle does

Naming clues

Ch11 s14

  • Names come from appearance, size, fiber orientation, position, function, bones attached, number of origins
  • Rectus = straight
  • Abdominis = abdominal area
  • Abductor = abducts
  • Oblique = at an angle
  • Platysma = flat sheet/plate (s28)
  • Sternocleidomastoid — named for its connections; cleido = clavicle

Motor unit size and precision

Ch11 s4

  • Fewer muscle cells in a motor unit → more precise movement
  • Eye muscles — extremely precise
  • Quadriceps — hundreds, very imprecise

Chapter 11 · Head, neck and trunk

Head and neck muscles

Ch11 s24–32

  • Orbicularis oculi — circles the eye; closes eyelids
  • Orbicularis oris — eating, drinking, speaking, kissing
  • Buccinator — compresses cheeks; “trumpet” muscle
  • Platysma — thin sheet over the anterior neck
  • Sternocleidomastoid — turns the head
  • Masseter — zygomatic arch → mandible; elevates mandible
  • Temporalis — similar to the masseter
Muscles of mastication = masseter and temporalis (they elevate the mandible).

Trunk muscles

Ch11 s34–40

  • Erector spinae — whole group of the vertebral column; straightens trunk and spine
  • Diaphragm — primary respiratory muscle. Connections: xiphoid, lower ribs, upper lumbar spine; central tendon. Action: flattens, expands thoracic volume
  • Intercostals (internal and external) — between the ribs
  • Rectus abdominis — pubic bone → lower ribs and xiphoid; flexes trunk, compresses abdomen
  • External and internal obliques
  • Linea alba — connective tissue line in the abdominal midline; divides the rectus abdominis

Chapter 11 · Shoulder, arm, hip and leg

Shoulder and arm

Ch11 s42, s47

  • Trapezius: occipital bone + cervical vertebrae → clavicle + scapular spine. Elevates, rotates or lowers scapula
  • Pectoralis major: sternum + clavicle → humerus. Flexes and adducts the arm
  • Latissimus dorsi: thoracic + lumbar vertebrae → humerus. Extends and adducts the arm
  • Serratus anterior: fixes/rotates the scapula
  • Biceps brachii: scapula → radius. Flexes elbow
  • Triceps brachii: humerus + scapula → ulna. Extends elbow

Rotator cuff (4 muscles)

Ch11 s47

  • Teres minor
  • Supraspinatus
  • Infraspinatus
  • Subscapularis
Mine, not the slide’s: the initials spell SITS (Supraspinatus, Infraspinatus, Teres minor, Subscapularis).

Anterior thigh

Ch11 s52

  • Sartorius and iliopsoas — flex the thigh at the hip; sartorius is the longest muscle in the body
  • Quadriceps femoris (4): rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, vastus intermedius. Pelvis → tibia via the patellar ligament. Extends the knee

Adductors, gluteals, hamstrings

Ch11 s54, s56

  • Adductors: adductor magnus, other adductors, gracilis — all adduct the thigh
  • Gluteals (3): maximus, medius, minimus. Pelvis + sacrum → iliotibial tract. Extend or abduct the thigh
  • Hamstrings (3): semitendinosus, semimembranosus, biceps femoris. Ischial tuberosity + femur → fibula + tibia. Flex the leg at the knee

Leg and foot

Ch11 s60

  • Tibialis anterior: tibia → 1st metatarsal. Dorsiflexes the foot
  • Gastrocnemius and soleus: femur → calcaneus (as the slide lists them). Plantar flex the foot
